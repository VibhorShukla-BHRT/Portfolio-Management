ABSTRACT & EXECUTIVE SUMMARY 
Modern computational asset management faces a fundamental dichotomy between two information 
modalities: unstructured qualitative narratives (corporate earnings filings, central bank statements, 
management commentary, and breaking geopolitical developments) and structured numerical metrics 
(trailing price histories, volatility surfaces, and empirical covariance matrices). Traditional quantitative 
models (e.g., Markowitz Mean-Variance Optimization, Kalman filters, and Deep Reinforcement Learning) 
excel at numerical optimization but remain structurally blind to qualitative nuance. Conversely, naive 
Large Language Model (LLM) agents possess rich natural language comprehension but exhibit 
catastrophic failure modes when applied directly to portfolio construction, including autoregressive 
hallucination (weights failing to sum to unity), covariance blindness (reckless concentration in correlated 
high-beta assets), promotional sycophancy, temporal amnesia, and parametric look-ahead bias. 
This project introduces the Adversarial Debate & Convex Optimization Portfolio (AD-COP) system, an 
institutional-grade autonomous asset management architecture that strictly decouples qualitative 
cognitive debate from deterministic convex optimization. Under AD-COP, fundamental equity analysis is 
executed through an adversarial debate topology comprising seven specialized AI agents (Universe 
Screener, Quantitative Analyst, Bull Researcher, Bear Researcher, Debate Orchestrator, Risk Manager, 
and Portfolio Manager). The dialectical consensus is translated into structured Bayesian views, which are 
ingested by convex quadratic solvers—specifically Black-Litterman (BL) equilibrium updating and 
Hierarchical Risk Parity (HRP) machine learning tree clustering—guaranteeing strict mathematical 
bounds and covariance calibration. Cognitive persistence is maintained through a local layered vector 
memory engine (ChromaDB with 384-dimensional all-MiniLM-L6-v2 embeddings) utilizing multi-tier 
exponential recency decay. 
To resolve the widespread vulnerability of parametric look-ahead contamination in LLM benchmarks, we 
introduce the DECONTAM Masking Protocol, enforcing bidirectional entity anonymization (e.g., TSLA to 
Asset_Alpha) and relative temporal indexation. Empirical evaluation was conducted over the identical 6-
month test window (November 1, 2022 to May 1, 2023) and identical 5-stock universe (TSLA, MSFT, 
AMZN, NFLX, COIN) established in benchmark literature by FinMem (Yu et al., Columbia/Peking 
University, IEEE TKDE 2023). Under unmasked comparative testing (Experiment 1), the system achieved 
a +40.74% discrete return, +30.94% continuous daily time-series return, and an annualized Sharpe ratio 
of 1.56 (outperforming FinMem's 1.42, S&P 500's 0.82, and Equal-Weight's 0.28). Under strict blind 
decontamination (Experiment 2), the system delivered +24.45% discrete return (+21.17% alpha over 
Equal-Weight), a Sharpe ratio of 0.77, and limited maximum drawdown to -17.11%. The ablation delta 
between Experiment 1 and Experiment 2 empirically isolates and quantifies the +16.29% Parametric 
Look-Ahead Premium present in prior unmasked literature, establishing an original, methodologically 
sound blueprint for autonomous financial intelligence.

CHAPTER 1: INTRODUCTION & PROBLEM FORMULATION 
1.1 The Algorithmic Asset Allocation Dilemma 
Modern computational financial markets are characterized by the continuous generation and 
aggregation of massive data streams across two fundamentally distinct information modalities: 
• 1. Unstructured Qualitative Information: Encompasses central bank monetary policy communiqués, 
Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC) minutes, regulatory corporate filings (SEC Forms 10-K, 10-
Q, 8-K), quarterly earnings conference call transcripts, executive management sentiment, patent 
litigation announcements, and high-velocity geopolitical news feeds. This data modality conveys the 
narrative, causal, and forward-looking drivers of corporate profitability and systemic risk. 
• 2. Structured Numerical Information: Encompasses high-frequency tick data, end-of-day Open-
High-Low-Close-Volume (OHLCV) series, implied volatility surfaces, trailing return covariance 
matrices, order book depth metrics, and macroeconomic indicator time-series. This data modality 
provides the rigorous statistical and numerical geometry necessary to quantify volatility, correlation, 
and downside risk. 
Historically, quantitative finance has relied on mathematical optimization models rooted in Modern 
Portfolio Theory (Markowitz, 1952), Capital Asset Pricing Model equilibrium (Sharpe, 1964), econometric 
volatility forecasting (ARCH/GARCH), and more recently, Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) algorithms 
such as Deep Q-Networks (DQN), Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), and Advantage Actor-Critic (A2C). 
While these numerical frameworks excel at processing structured historical matrices, they are inherently 
blind to qualitative narratives. A statistical optimizer cannot parse the nuanced rhetoric of a Federal 
Reserve Chairman hinting at an impending liquidity contraction, nor can it evaluate the competitive 
ramifications of an antitrust investigation until the resulting price collapse is already reflected in 
historical returns. 
Conversely, the emergence of Large Language Models (LLMs) equipped with tens of billions of 
parameters has demonstrated astonishing semantic reasoning, contextual inference, and zero-shot 
textual comprehension. However, when financial practitioners attempt to deploy naive LLM agents 
directly into live trading and portfolio allocation workflows, they encounter severe and catastrophic 
structural failure modes that render direct LLM execution dangerous. 
1.2 Catastrophic Failure Modes of Naive Financial LLMs 
Empirical investigation reveals five primary failure modes when autoregressive language models are 
prompted directly to manage financial capital: 
1.2.1 Autoregressive Hallucination and Constraint Violation 
LLMs are probabilistic token-prediction engines trained to minimize cross-entropy loss over text tokens. 
They possess no native comprehension of physical balance sheets, budget constraints, or numerical 
equalities. When prompted with a query such as "Allocate $100,000 across TSLA, MSFT, AMZN, NFLX, 
and COIN," standard models invariably hallucinate allocation percentages that fail to sum to unity (i.e.,

maximum single-asset limits). In backtesting simulations, unconstrained LLM outputs frequently assign

1.2.2 Covariance Blindness and High-Beta Overconcentration 
A foundational tenet of modern portfolio management is that an asset must never be evaluated in 
isolation, but strictly by its marginal contribution to overall portfolio variance (w^T * Sigma * w). LLMs 
lack an internal linear algebra solver and cannot compute eigenvalues or covariance structures. 
Consequently, when presented with bullish narratives across multiple technology equities, a naive agent 
allocates capital based on narrative excitement rather than diversification. In a market rally, this causes 
massive overconcentration in highly correlated, high-beta assets (e.g., allocating heavily to TSLA, COIN, 
and NVDA simultaneously), creating catastrophic vulnerability to drawdowns during sector-wide 
liquidity shocks. 
1.2.3 Sycophancy and Corporate PR Promotional Framing Bias 
Corporate press releases, earnings calls, and financial marketing materials are engineered to project 
optimism and deflect scrutiny. Autoregressive models exhibit a documented behavioral pathology 
known as 'sycophancy'—they adopt the emotional tone and framing of their input context. When an 
agent reads an earnings report celebrating 'record user growth' while burying expanding operating 
losses in the notes, a single-prompt LLM adopts a promotional framing bias, systematically 
underestimating tail-risk, debt rollover vulnerabilities, and margin deterioration. 
1.2.4 Temporal Amnesia and Tabula Rasa Inference 
Standard LLM API calls are stateless. Each trading rebalancing prompt is evaluated tabula rasa, with zero 
persistent memory of previous market crashes, volatility spikes, or prior forecasting mistakes. When 
sudden macroeconomic shifts occur (e.g., the Silicon Valley Bank collapse in March 2023), a stateless 
agent treats the event as a completely novel phenomenon, failing to draw analogies to historical 
liquidity squeezes or recall how defensive cash-flow assets performed in past cycles. 
1.2.5 Parametric Look-Ahead Contamination 
The academic evaluation of LLMs on historical financial data suffers from rampant methodological flaws. 
Modern open-source models (such as Llama 3, Qwen 2.5, Mistral, and DeepSeek) were pre-trained on 
internet crawls spanning through 2023 and 2024. When an agent is evaluated on historical 2022 market 
data with prompts mentioning 'Tesla' or 'Microsoft', the model's internal weights have already 
memorized that Elon Musk acquired Twitter in late 2022 causing TSLA to drop 45%, or that Microsoft 
invested $10B in OpenAI in early 2023 causing MSFT to surge. This creates artificial, unreplicable 'hall of 
mirrors' alpha driven by parametric memorization rather than genuine analytical inference.

2.1 Modern Portfolio Theory & Mean-Variance Optimization 
Modern Portfolio Theory (MPT), formulated by Harry Markowitz in his seminal 1952 paper, laid the 
mathematical foundation of quantitative asset allocation. Markowitz demonstrated that an investor can 
construct an 'efficient frontier' of optimal portfolios offering the maximum possible expected return for 
a given level of risk. 
Consider a universe of N risky financial assets. Let w = [w_1, w_2, ..., w_N]^T denote the vector of 
portfolio weights, where w_i represents the proportion of capital allocated to asset i. The budget 
constraint requires that all capital be allocated: 
sum(w_i) = w^T * 1 = 1.0,    subject to w_i >= 0 (for long-only mandates)    (2.1) 
Let mu = [mu_1, mu_2, ..., mu_N]^T denote the vector of expected asset returns, and let Sigma in R^(N x 
N) denote the positive semi-definite covariance matrix of asset returns. The expected portfolio return 
E(R_p) and portfolio variance sigma_p^2 are expressed as: 
E(R_p) = w^T * mu = sum(w_i * mu_i)    (2.2) 
sigma_p^2 = w^T * Sigma * w = sum_i sum_j w_i * w_j * sigma_ij    (2.3) 
The risk-adjusted performance of the portfolio is quantified by the Sharpe Ratio (Sharpe, 1966), which 
measures excess return per unit of total volatility relative to an annualized risk-free rate R_f: 
Sharpe Ratio = (E(R_p) - R_f) / sigma_p = (w^T * mu - R_f) / sqrt(w^T * Sigma * w)    (2.4) 
2.1.1 The Markowitz Error Maximization Failure Mode 
While mathematically elegant, classic Mean-Variance Optimization (MVO) exhibits severe practical 
failure modes. Traditional MVO requires inverting the sample covariance matrix (Sigma^-1). When 
estimated over finite empirical return windows, sample covariance matrices contain substantial 
statistical estimation error. Inverting Sigma acts as an 'error maximizer' (Michaud, 1989): the optimizer 
assigns massive long positions to assets whose returns were artificially inflated by noise, and aggressive 
short or zero positions to assets whose risks were overestimated. In live out-of-sample trading, naive 
MVO portfolios frequently experience extreme drawdowns and erratic turnover. 
2.2 The CAPM Equilibrium Prior Formulation 
To mitigate the noise inherent in historical mean estimates, William Sharpe (1964) and John Lintner 
(1965) developed the Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM). Under market-clearing equilibrium 
assumptions, aggregate demand equals aggregate supply, meaning the aggregate market capitalization 
portfolio w_mkt is mean-variance efficient.

By reversing the Markowitz optimization problem, one can extract the 'implied equilibrium excess 
return vector' Pi. If all investors hold the market portfolio, the returns required to clear the market must 
satisfy: 
Pi = lambda * Sigma * w_mkt    (2.5) 
Where lambda represents the aggregate market risk-aversion parameter, mathematically formulated as: 
lambda = (E(R_m) - R_f) / sigma_m^2    (2.6) 
In Eq. 2.6, E(R_m) is the expected return of the broad market index (e.g., S&P 500), and sigma_m^2 is 
the market variance. The equilibrium return vector Pi represents a neutral, objective, and well-
diversified baseline prior. If an investor possesses zero private information or views, holding weights 
proportional to Pi guarantees market-level diversification without erratic allocation extremes. 
2.3 The Black-Litterman Model: Bayesian Generalized Least Squares 
Fischer Black and Robert Litterman (1991, 1992) revolutionized quantitative asset allocation by 
integrating Bayesian statistics with modern portfolio theory. Recognizing that investors frequently 
possess valuable qualitative insights on specific assets but lack reliable forecasts for the entire universe, 
Black-Litterman combines the objective CAPM equilibrium prior Pi with subjective investor views Q, 
weighted by view uncertainty Omega. 
Let K be the number of views expressed on N assets. Investor views are modeled as a linear system with 
Gaussian error: 
P * mu = Q + epsilon,    epsilon ~ N(0, Omega)    (2.7) 
Where: 
• P is a K x N 'pick matrix' identifying the assets targeted by each view. 
• Q is a K x 1 vector containing the expected return of each view. 
• Omega is a K x K diagonal covariance matrix representing the degree of uncertainty in each view. 
Smaller diagonal entries indicate high investor confidence, while larger values indicate high uncertainty. 
• tau is a scalar calibration parameter (typically set between 0.025 and 0.05) representing the 
uncertainty of the prior equilibrium distribution relative to sample covariance. 
Using Generalized Least Squares (GLS) and Bayes' Theorem, the posterior expected return distribution 
E(R) is formulated as: 
E(R) = [ (tau * Sigma)^-1 + P^T * Omega^-1 * P ]^-1 * [ (tau * Sigma)^-1 * Pi + P^T * Omega^-1 * Q 
]    (2.8) 
The corresponding posterior covariance matrix M is computed as: 
M = Sigma + [ (tau * Sigma)^-1 + P^T * Omega^-1 * P ]^-1    (2.9) 
The elegance of the Black-Litterman model lies in its mathematical safety properties: if the investor 
expresses no view on a particular asset, that asset smoothly and automatically reverts to its CAPM 
market equilibrium weight. Estimation noise is dramatically suppressed, producing stable, institutional-
grade portfolio weights.

2.4 Hierarchical Risk Parity (HRP) Graph Clustering 
While the Black-Litterman model solves the mean estimation dilemma, it still relies on quadratic 
programming over the covariance matrix Sigma. During severe market crises (such as the liquidity 
freezes observed in Q4 2022 and March 2023), empirical covariance matrices become ill-conditioned, 
with condition numbers kappa(Sigma) = lambda_max / lambda_min exceeding 10^3. In extreme cases, 
collinearity renders Sigma non-invertible, causing quadratic solvers to fail. 
To provide a mathematically guaranteed fallback, Marcos Lopez de Prado (2016) introduced Hierarchical 
Risk Parity (HRP). HRP completely circumvents matrix inversion by leveraging machine learning graph 
theory and hierarchical tree clustering across three distinct algorithmic phases: 
• Phase 1: Tree Clustering: Transforms the Pearson correlation coefficient rho_ij between assets into 
a proper metric distance: d_ij = sqrt(0.5 * (1 - rho_ij)). Assets with identical correlation have distance 
0; completely uncorrelated assets have distance 1. Hierarchical linkage clustering (single linkage or 
Ward's method) is applied to group assets into a hierarchical dendrogram. 
• Phase 2: Quasi-Diagonalization: Reorganizes the rows and columns of the covariance matrix Sigma 
so that correlated assets are positioned adjacent to one another along the principal diagonal. This 
groups similar risk factors together. 
• Phase 3: Recursive Bisection: Recursively bisects the dendrogram top-down. For two sub-clusters 
C_1 and C_2 with cluster variances V_1 and V_2 (where V = w^T * Sigma_tilde * w), capital is 
allocated inversely proportional to cluster variance: 
alpha_1 = 1 - (V_1 / (V_1 + V_2)),    alpha_2 = 1 - alpha_1 
This recursive bisection continues until individual asset weights are finalized. 
HRP guarantees that capital is diversified across independent structural risk dimensions rather than 
individual correlated equities. Because HRP requires zero matrix inversion, it never crashes, never 
produces negative variance allocations, and remains mathematically robust even during extreme market 
dislocation.

multivariate portfolio, cannot model asset covariance Sigma, and cannot execute cross-asset risk

4.1 Cognitive Memory Taxonomy: Working, Intermediate, and Deep 
Human cognitive architectures do not store all experiential stimuli within a single homogeneous 
memory buffer. As established in cognitive neuroscience (Atkinson & Shiffrin, 1968) and adapted to 
computational finance by Yu et al. (2023), effective decision-making requires a multi-tier memory 
taxonomy that separates fleeting market rumors from structural macro shifts. 
The AD-COP framework organizes cognitive persistence into three distinct operational tiers, 
implemented locally using ChromaDB: 
• 1. Shallow Layer (High-Frequency Operational Memory): Assigned an analyst importance score of 1 
to 3. Captures intraday market rumors, breaking headlines, and high-frequency price fluctuations. 
This layer is designed to decay rapidly so that ephemeral news does not corrupt medium-term 
strategic allocation. 
• 2. Intermediate Layer (Tactical Fundamental Memory): Assigned an analyst importance score of 4 
to 7. Stores quarterly Form 10-Q filings, earnings surprise percentages, management revenue 
guidance revisions, and intermediate technical moving average crossovers. Its retention cycle is 
calibrated to match the 90-day corporate reporting cycle. 
• 3. Deep Layer (Strategic Macro Regime Memory): Assigned an analyst importance score of 8 to 10. 
Stores comprehensive Form 10-K annual audits, systemic liquidity contractions (e.g., Federal 
Reserve rate hike cycles, banking collapses), and major corporate restructurings. This layer exhibits 
near-permanent retention to provide analog context during extreme volatility regimes.

CHAPTER 5: CONTAMINATION CONTROL: THE DECONTAM 
MASKING PROTOCOL 
5.1 The Threat of Parametric Look-Ahead Memorization 
A profound vulnerability threatening the validity of financial machine learning literature is parametric 
look-ahead contamination. Modern frontier LLMs are pre-trained on terabytes of public text, including 
financial news websites, historical SEC filings, and financial market recaps through 2023–2024. If an 
agent backtested on Q4 2022 is prompted with real company names (e.g., 'Tesla' or 'Coinbase'), it does 
not need to analyze balance sheet debt or moving averages—its neural weights have already memorized 
that Elon Musk sold billions in Tesla shares to finance Twitter and that FTX collapsed in November 2022, 
causing TSLA and COIN to plummet. 
Any trading performance reported under unmasked conditions is therefore compromised by memorized 
hindsight bias. To produce scientifically defensible, publishable results, an autonomous trading 
architecture must enforce strict information isolation. 
5.2 Multi-Modal Masking Architecture: Textual & Numerical 
The DECONTAM Masking Protocol, implemented in simulation.py, enforces comprehensive, 
bidirectional anonymization across both information modalities before any data reaches the language 
model: 
• 1. Entity Anonymization: A deterministic regular-expression mapping engine transforms all equity 
ticker symbols and corporate names into Greek-letter pseudonyms: TSLA / Tesla -> Asset_Alpha,

6.1 PyPortfolioOpt Convex Optimization Backend 
The deterministic optimization backend is implemented in tools/optimization_tools.py using the open-

6.2 Black-Litterman Execution Pipeline & Fault-Tolerant JSON Parsing 
The execution pipeline follows four rigorous sequential stages: 
• Stage 1: Fault-Tolerant Ingestion: The Debate Orchestrator's markdown output is parsed to extract 
the views JSON string. To protect against malformed JSON or unescaped strings generated by LLMs, 
the system employs the json_repair library alongside regex fallback extractors, guaranteeing zero 
crashes from syntax anomalies. 
• Stage 2: Prior Matrix Ingestion: The genuine 2-year trailing historical price matrix ending at 
SIMULATED_DATE is downloaded via yfinance. The sample covariance matrix Sigma is estimated, 
and the CAPM equilibrium excess return prior Pi is computed via capm_return(data). 
• Stage 3: Bayesian Synthesis: The subjective expected return vector Q is constructed from the 
parsed views. PyPortfolioOpt's BlackLittermanModel(Sigma, pi=Pi, absolute_views=Q, tau=0.05) 
computes the posterior expected return vector E(R) and posterior covariance matrix M. 
• Stage 4: Sharpe Ratio Optimization: The posterior returns E(R) and covariance M are passed to 
EfficientFrontier(E(R), M). The solver maximizes the Sharpe Ratio subject to full investment sum(w_i) 
= 1.0 and long-only bounds w_i in [0, 1]. Weights are cleaned and formatted into a markdown table.

7.4 Quantitative Evaluation Metrics & Formulations 
All performance metrics are calculated deterministically by backtest_engine.py using continuous daily 
price series downloaded via yfinance: 
1. Discrete Compounded Return R_discrete: The product of realized returns across the three rebalancing 
sub-periods: 
   R_discrete = prod_{k=1}^3 (1 + R_k) - 1.0 
 
2. Continuous Daily Time-Series Return R_daily: Computed from daily equity curves where weights are 
held constant within each sub-period: 
   R_daily = prod_{t=1}^T (1 + r_t) - 1.0 
 
3. Realized Daily Volatility sigma_daily and Annualized Volatility sigma_annual: 
   sigma_daily = std(r_t),    sigma_annual = sigma_daily * sqrt(252) 
 
4. Annualized Sharpe Ratio (with annualized risk-free rate R_f = 4.0%): 
   Sharpe = sqrt(252) * [ mean(r_t - (R_f / 252)) ] / sigma_daily

def mask_text(text: str) -> str: 
    if not _MASKING_ACTIVE or not text: 
        return text

11.1 Critical Synthesis of Findings 
The empirical findings of this research validate our foundational hypothesis: Large Language Models are 
profoundly valuable in computational asset management, but strictly when confined to qualitative 
semantic extraction and dialectical synthesis. By relieving the LLM of the mathematical burden of weight 
calculation and delegating portfolio construction to convex quadratic solvers (Black-Litterman and HRP), 
the AD-COP architecture achieves superior risk-adjusted returns (Sharpe 1.56 vs. FinMem's 1.42) while 
strictly guaranteeing mathematical feasibility. 
Furthermore, our ablation study successfully exposes the magnitude of parametric look-ahead bias 
(+16.29%) inherent in contemporary unmasked literature. Without the DECONTAM Masking Protocol, 
published trading agents appear artificially prescient because they exploit historical training data. 
11.2 Structural Limitations of the Current Prototype 
While highly effective as an academic proof-of-concept, the current architecture possesses several 
structural limitations that must be addressed before institutional production deployment: 
• 1. Discrete Rebalancing Frequency: The current backtest executes rebalancing at discrete 2-month 
intervals. In real-world trading, high-impact catalysts (e.g., emergency Fed rate announcements, 
bank failures) occur asynchronously, requiring dynamic continuous rebalancing triggers. 
• 2. Absence of Market Impact & Slippage Models: The simulation assumes zero execution slippage 
and zero broker commissions. In institutional environments, portfolio turnover incurs bid-ask 
friction and market impact costs that penalize excessive reallocation. 
• 3. Static Universe Scale: The core benchmark was conducted across a 5-equity universe to maintain 
identical parity with FinMem. Real-world institutional portfolios mandate scanning hundreds of 
assets across global sectors.

APPENDIX A: COMPLETE MATHEMATICAL DERIVATIONS 
A.1 Generalized Least Squares Derivation of the Black-Litterman Formula 
The Black-Litterman model formulates expected asset returns as a combination of two distinct sources 
of information: 
1. The market equilibrium distribution: mu ~ N(Pi, tau * Sigma) 
2. The subjective investor/agent views: P * mu = Q + epsilon, where epsilon ~ N(0, Omega) 
We can express these two equations in a unified stacked Generalized Least Squares (GLS) regression 
model: 
y_stacked = X_stacked * mu + epsilon_stacked    (A.1) 
Where: 
y_stacked = [ Pi ; Q ],    X_stacked = [ I_N ; P ],    V_stacked = [ tau * Sigma, 0 ; 0, Omega ] 
The Generalized Least Squares estimator minimizes the Mahalanobis distance: 
S(mu) = (y_stacked - X_stacked * mu)^T * V_stacked^-1 * (y_stacked - X_stacked * mu) 
Taking the matrix derivative with respect to mu and setting it to zero: 
d S(mu) / d mu = -2 * X_stacked^T * V_stacked^-1 * (y_stacked - X_stacked * mu) = 0 
X_stacked^T * V_stacked^-1 * X_stacked * mu = X_stacked^T * V_stacked^-1 * y_stacked    (A.2) 
Substituting the block matrix components: 
X_stacked^T * V_stacked^-1 * X_stacked = [ I_N^T , P^T ] * [ (tau * Sigma)^-1, 0 ; 0, Omega^-1 ] * [ I_N ; 
P ] 
                                     = (tau * Sigma)^-1 + P^T * Omega^-1 * P 
Similarly, for the right-hand side: 
X_stacked^T * V_stacked^-1 * y_stacked = (tau * Sigma)^-1 * Pi + P^T * Omega^-1 * Q 
Inverting the matrix yields the definitive Black-Litterman master posterior formula for E(R): 
E(R) = [ (tau * Sigma)^-1 + P^T * Omega^-1 * P ]^-1 * [ (tau * Sigma)^-1 * Pi + P^T * Omega^-1 * Q 
]    (A.3) 
A.2 Hierarchical Risk Parity: Cluster Variance and Recursive Bisection 
Let C be an asset cluster containing assets indexed by i = 1, ..., N_c with covariance sub-matrix Sigma_C. 
Under inverse-variance allocation, the weights w_tilde within the cluster are proportional to inverse 
marginal variance: 
w_tilde_i = (1 / sigma_ii^2) / sum_{j in C} (1 / sigma_jj^2)    (A.4) 
The aggregate synthetic variance of cluster C is given by the quadratic form: 
V_C = w_tilde^T * Sigma_C * w_tilde    (A.5)
